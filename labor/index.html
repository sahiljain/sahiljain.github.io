    • contractions are regular and getting closer (for example, every 5 minutes for an hour),
    • the baby is moving less than usual, or you just feel something is wrong.

    How the estimate works

    The timing of spontaneous labor is modelled as a smooth distribution of pregnancy length centred a few days past the due date for a first baby (about 40w3d) and a day past it for later babies (about 40w1d), with a small early tail for preterm labor. Its spread depends on how the due date was set: an early ultrasound or IVF date pins it down better than a last period alone.

    Personal factors nudge the centre by a day or a few: your last labor's timing (pregnancy lengths are moderately correlated, so about a third of the difference carries over), cycle length when dated by period, age, BMI and ancestry. A previous preterm birth enlarges the early tail.

    Things happening now — cervical dilation and effacement, show, a recent sweep — multiply the day-to-day chance of labor for a week or two, then fade back. The whole picture is then conditioned on labor not having started yet, so the odds update as each day passes. A scheduled induction or C-section ends the forecast on that date.

    The baseline figures follow large studies of spontaneous labor timing (for example Mittendorf et al. 1990, Smith 2001, Jukic et al. 2013), ethnic differences in gestation length (Patel et al. 2004), recurrence of gestation length between pregnancies, and Cochrane reviews of membrane sweeping. Effect sizes are rounded and simplified, so read the results as a reasonable guide rather than a clinical prediction. Twins in particular are a rough sketch.
